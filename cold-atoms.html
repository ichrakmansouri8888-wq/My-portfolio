04 — TEAM PROJECT 2024 – 2025

Automated Characterization of a Cloud of Cold Rubidium Atoms

Institut d'Optique

A magneto-optical trap cools and confines rubidium atoms with laser light alone: a beam tuned slightly below an atomic transition slows any atom moving toward it through the Doppler effect, and a magnetic-field gradient turns that into a real spatial trap through Zeeman shifting. The goal was twofold: rebuild a cold rubidium cloud on top of a previous year's setup, with better laser locking and a reorganized optical table, then characterize it quantitatively, its temperature and density, through time-of-flight absorption imaging.

Four things had to come together to make a real measurement: two lasers locked the right way, a trap with the right field and polarization, a frequency doubler that could survive contact with the rest of the table, and a fully automated way to trigger and analyze every shot. Each is broken out below.

01

Laser Locking & Trapping

1 / 3

Two lasers, two lock schemes : I locked the repump laser first, using saturated absorption spectroscopy on the rubidium spectrum and a modulation based dispersion technique to build an error signal, then held it at the transition's zero crossing with a PID. The cooling laser came next, locked instead to the beat frequency between the two beams, servoed to a target detuning of 1.48 GHz. The trapping field itself came from two coils in an anti-Helmholtz configuration at 3 A, and circular polarization on each beam arm, verified against an ellipticity above 39 on a polarization analyzer, gave the correct angular momentum selection rules for trapping.

02

PPLN Failure & Rebuild

1 / 3

Diagnosis, redesign, re-characterization : Partway through the project, the PPLN crystal that doubles our cooling laser's frequency failed and stopped the experiment. I diagnosed the failure, then designed a dedicated, temperature-controlled protective housing for the doubler in SolidWorks, isolated from the rest of the table so a routine adjustment elsewhere could never damage it again. Once it was back in place, I re-characterized its output beam from scratch, fitting the transverse intensity profile with an erf function at two distances to confirm the mode was still a clean Gaussian and recover its new waist, before folding it back into a reorganized table with room for the imaging line that was wanted to be added from the start.

03

Automated Sequencing

1 / 3

One brain, four dedicated slaves : Each time-of-flight data point needs a freshly recreated cloud, released and imaged at a precise, independently chosen delay. I built a trigger architecture around one Arduino DUE acting as the brain, fanning out to four Arduino UNOs, one each for two shutters, the AOM driver and the camera, sequencing the whole cool, release, wait, image cycle to microsecond precision no one could reproduce by hand. I considered an I2C bus for the same job, but kept the simpler dedicated trigger lines once they proved more reliable on the bench.

04

Data Acquisition & Analysis

1 / 3

From pixels to temperature : I wrote a Python and Tkinter tool, the Image Line Analyzer, that pulls vertical and horizontal intensity profiles out of an absorption image. Fitting how the cloud's width grows with delay time against σ(t)² = (kT/m)t² + σ₀² gives the temperature directly, a pipeline I validated first on synthetically generated Gaussian test images before trusting it on real time-of-flight data.
