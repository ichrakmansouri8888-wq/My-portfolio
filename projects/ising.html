Hexagonal 2D Ising Model: Antiferromagnetic Frustration

Université Paris-Saclay

The Ising model is one of statistical physics' simplest descriptions of magnetism, spins on a lattice, each pointing up or down, interacting only with their nearest neighbors, and yet it is rich enough that Onsager's 1944 solution of the 2D square case is a landmark result. On a square lattice, antiferromagnetic coupling settles neatly into an alternating checkerboard. On a hexagonal lattice it cannot: every triangular plaquette carries three antiferromagnetic bonds, and no arrangement can satisfy all three at once. In this context, I built a Metropolis Monte Carlo simulation, N = 100 spins on an L = 10 hexagonal lattice with periodic boundary conditions, to see exactly what that geometric frustration does to the physics.

The build breaks into four pieces below: the lattice and why it frustrates, a ferromagnetic run on the same geometry as a control, the frustrated case set directly against an unfrustrated square lattice I added myself, and finally what happens when an external field is thrown into the competition.

01

Lattice & Frustration

1 / 3

Six neighbors, not four : Forcing all three bonds of a triangular plaquette to be antiferromagnetic requires (s₁s₂)(s₂s₃)(s₃s₁) = −1, but that same product also equals s₁²s₂²s₃² = 1, a direct contradiction. Every triangle is stuck with two satisfied bonds and one frustrated one, the "two up, one down" motif boxed above. I wrote the red/blue sphere renderer myself, specifically so a geometric effect like this would be something you could see directly, rather than infer from a plot. The consequence goes beyond any single triangle: because many different spin arrangements tie for that same lowest energy, the lattice never settles on one ordered ground state the way the ferromagnet does, it stays macroscopically degenerate, with a finite residual entropy even as the temperature goes to zero.

02

Ferromagnetic Baseline

1 / 3

The same lattice, without frustration : Before touching the antiferromagnetic case, I ran the ferromagnetic version on the identical hexagonal geometry as a control, validated against Wannier's 1950 analytic solution for the triangular Ising net. It behaves exactly as theory predicts: full order at low temperature, a sharp heat capacity peak marking a genuine second-order transition, and a clean collapse into disorder above it, which confirmed the simulation itself was correct before frustration entered the picture at all.

03

Frustration vs. Square Lattice

1 / 6

Isolating the frustration effect : The ground-state energy on the frustrated hexagonal lattice settles at only about a third of the fully ordered ferromagnetic value, direct evidence that a third of the bonds can never be satisfied. I added the square-lattice antiferromagnetic run myself, same Hamiltonian, same Metropolis code, only the geometry changed, specifically to isolate this effect: remove the frustration and the system falls straight into a perfect checkerboard ground state, its energy and magnetization curves both smooth and well-behaved, with a sharp heat capacity peak restored right at the Néel temperature. That contrast is what actually proves the effect is geometric frustration, and not just "antiferromagnetic coupling" in general.

04

Magnetic Field Response

1 / 6

Three energy terms in competition. Starting from a disordered paramagnetic state at T* = 2 and B* = 0, the physics here comes down to a competition between three distinct energetic terms: the Zeeman energy, which tends to align the spins with the field B*, the antiferromagnetic exchange interaction, which tends to anti-align neighboring spins since the exchange constant J is positive, and thermal agitation, which tends to disorder the system. A moderate field up to B* = 6 only pulls the magnetization to about 0.6, still short of the ideal paramagnet's theoretical law M* = tanh(B*/T*), which predicts 0.995 for these parameters, evidence of how strongly the system resists. Only at B* ≈ 20, twenty times the local interaction energy, does the magnetic energy finally overwhelm the frustration and force the lattice into complete, field-imposed order.
